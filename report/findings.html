Do Congressional Traders Generate Risk-Adjusted Alpha?

Two independent selection hypotheses, tested out-of-sample on 33,522 disclosed trades. Neither survives the market factor.

Evaluation window 2020-01 → 2024-12 N = 60 months CAPM & Carhart 4-factor, Newey–West HAC

Finding

No detectable risk-adjusted alpha in either track. Members selected for past trading performance earned +3.49%/yr of CAPM alpha out-of-sample, but the estimate is statistically indistinguishable from zero and its sign reverses under alternative split dates. Members selected for committee oversight, tested only in the sectors they oversee, earned −5.34%/yr — also indistinguishable from zero.

Both portfolios load on the market at β ≈ 1 and the market explains 87–90% of their variance. What looks like stock-picking is, to the precision this data supports, market exposure.

The question

Members of Congress must disclose their personal stock trades. Do any of them beat the market once you strip out the market itself?

The interesting question is not whether some member's portfolio went up — in 2020–2024 almost everything went up. It is whether any group earned a return the market factor cannot explain. That is what α measures, and separating it from β is the entire exercise.

Two groups are worth testing, for different reasons, and both are tested the same way — selection in one window, evaluation in a later, non-overlapping one:

  • Track A — performance selection. Rank members on trading performance in a formation window (2014–2019), then test the top quartile in 2020–2024. This asks whether past winners persist, which is the difference between skill and luck.
  • Track B — committee oversight. Select members by an ex-ante characteristic — which committee they sat on — and test them only in the sectors that committee regulates. Selection never touches returns at all, so this group cannot have been chosen for being lucky.

How the test avoids fooling itself

Four rules, enforced by assertions in code rather than by good intentions.

RULE 01

Enter on the disclosure date

Filings lag the trade by a median of 29 days — and 438 days at the 95th percentile. Entering on the trade date would credit the portfolio with a price move that was neither public nor investable. This is the single largest look-ahead trap in this literature.

RULE 02

Quarantine selection from evaluation

Track A ranks on formation months only; a hard assertion fails the run if an evaluation month leaks in. The validation suite independently re-derives the selected group from formation data alone and confirms it reproduces exactly.

RULE 03

Make the exit carry no information

Positions exit exactly 12 months after entry — a fixed function of the entry date, so it cannot encode what the price did next. Holding until a disclosed closing trade was rejected: those are filed sparsely and late, which would make the exit date itself an informed choice.

RULE 04

Report every variant

Run enough specifications and one crosses t = 2 by chance. The defence is not fewer specifications — it is showing all 44 of them, so a starred cell can be read against the nulls surrounding it.

One deliberately conservative choice. A filing lands mid-month, so crediting that whole month's return would capture the part that preceded disclosure. Positions therefore earn from the following month. This can only reduce measured alpha, never inflate it — and the zero-lag variant is reported anyway, because if alpha appeared only there, it would be an artefact.

Track A — does past performance persist?

Of 259 members with disclosed trades, 30 cleared the pre-committed eligibility floor: at least 10 trades and 12 invested months during the formation window. Ranking those 30 on formation-period return and taking the top quartile selects 8 members — 5 Democrats, 3 Republicans, all House.

In formation, this group returned 23.9%/yr against 8.2%/yr for the pool they were drawn from. That gap is not evidence of anything: it is the selection. The question is what happened next.

Two details matter more than the headline. First, the control group works — the members the ranking rejected did no better, and their long–short leg is reliably negative. Second, and decisively: under alternative split dates the selected group's alpha changes sign. A real effect does not do that.

Track B — does oversight confer an edge?

Committee membership was reconstructed point-in-time for the 116th, 117th and 118th Congresses, then joined to trading members by name (99.6% matched, 258 of 259). Each member's trades were restricted to the GICS sectors their committee actually oversees, using a sector map fixed in advance with a written oversight rationale for every row.

That leaves 1,843 in-sector trades across 90 members. Samples are small by construction, so these results are reported with confidence intervals rather than t-statistics alone.

A formal era-interaction model puts the alpha shift at +6.07%/yr with p = 0.19 — not detectable. For Track B the beta shift is significant (p = 0.004) while the alpha shift is not, which is the general pattern: what changes across eras is market exposure, not skill.

Political regime is confounded with market conditions here — the COVID crash, the 2022 bear market. This is a question about stability across market regimes, and it should not be reported as a partisan claim.

Committee-map robustness

The committee→sector map is author-defined, so the obvious objection is that it was chosen to produce the answer. Re-running with the two most debatable assignments varied moves the combined estimate from −5.37% to −3.86% (Energy & Commerce reassigned to Health Care) and −3.64% (Real Estate dropped from Financial Services). All three remain insignificant. The conclusion survives the map.

The one significant positive result

Read this before quoting the Science/Commerce number

Across the main table, 44 specifications were estimated. Three cross the 5% threshold. Under the null of zero alpha everywhere, about 2.2 would be expected to do so by chance.

Two of the three are negative (the rejected-members control group). The single positive one — Science/Commerce under the 4-factor model, +6.78%/yr, t = 2.16 — does not survive its own CAPM specification (t = 1.80), rests on 463 trades by 24 members, and is exactly the kind of isolated cell this count predicts.

It is reported because every result is reported. It should not be read as evidence that the Science and Commerce committees confer a tradeable information advantage.

The rolling 24-month analysis tells the same story from another angle: across 37 overlapping windows for Track A, alpha stays in a −1.9% to +9.5% band and exactly one window reaches |t| > 2. Because the windows overlap heavily, those are not independent tests — the series is useful for shape, not for counting significances.

What the long–short leg shows

Treating disclosed sales as short positions collapses market exposure almost exactly as theory predicts — Track A's β falls from 1.03 to −0.18, Armed Services from 1.24 to −0.01. The hedge works.

But alpha does not survive the hedge. Track A's long–short alpha is −0.40%/yr (t = −0.09). Once market exposure is removed, essentially nothing remains. That is the cleanest statement the data supports: these portfolios were long the market, and the market was what paid them.

Limitations

  • Statistical power is the binding constraint. Sixty monthly observations and eight selected members cannot resolve a plausible alpha of 2–3%/yr. Most results here are better described as underpowered than as null.
  • Delisting bias, and it is not neutral. 617 traded tickers have no retrievable price history — 10.5% of in-span trades. Most were acquired or taken private, including names liquid throughout 2020–2024 whose history the price source no longer serves. Acquisition targets typically jump on announcement, so this most likely understates returns.
  • Disclosed amounts are ranges, so size-weighting relies on noisy midpoints. Equal-weighting is the default for that reason.
  • Sector labels are GICS-like, not licensed GICS, and reflect a current snapshot — a company reclassified since 2020 carries today's label.
  • The committee→sector map is author-defined. There is no dataset for it. Every row carries a written oversight rationale, and the map-robustness variants above exist precisely because this is a judgement call.
  • Broad committees resist clean mapping. Energy & Commerce alone touches energy, health and communications; its 64 in-sector trades yield a 70-point confidence interval.

Three things the build surfaced

Each of these would have silently corrupted the result, and each is the kind of failure that leaves a pipeline still appearing to run correctly.

  1. A sector-taxonomy mismatch nearly deleted two committee groups. The price source reports "Financial Services" and "Healthcare"; the sector map is written in GICS terms — "Financials", "Health Care". Five of ten sectors never matched, and the health and agriculture groups vanished entirely while everything still ran. A translation layer and a vocabulary assertion now make this class of failure loud.
  2. Point-in-time committee data does not exist upstream. The legislators dataset ships only a current membership snapshot, which would have tested 2020 trades against 2025 committee seats and dropped everyone who has since left Congress. Historical membership was reconstructed by pinning the file's git history to a commit inside each Congress.
  3. The eligibility floor excludes a specific cohort, for a good reason. Several high-volume traders show zero formation invested months — they are largely the January 2019 freshman class, whose trades cluster at the very end of the formation window. High trade counts do not give them a formation track record, and ranking them would score them on a few months of noise.

Conclusion

Neither selecting congressional traders on past performance nor selecting them on committee oversight produced detectable risk-adjusted alpha out-of-sample.

Track A's positive point estimate is not significant, reverses sign under alternative split dates, and disappears entirely once market exposure is hedged out. Track B is negative throughout and survives every variation of the sector map. The single significant positive cell in 44 specifications is what a multiple-comparisons count predicts.

The correct conclusion is not "members of Congress have no skill" — this design lacks the power to establish that. It is narrower and more defensible: on disclosed trades, entered when the public could actually have entered them, there is no evidence of returns the market factor cannot explain. The information advantage, if it exists, does not survive the disclosure lag.

Data. 68,167 disclosures → 33,522 trades after filtering (congress only, common stock, directional transactions, priced, valid dates). 259 members, 2,075 priced tickers. Committee data from @unitedstates/congress-legislators; factors from the Ken French Data Library; prices from Yahoo Finance, monthly adjusted close.

Method. CAPM and Carhart 4-factor regressions on monthly excess returns, Newey–West HAC standard errors at 6 lags to account for the overlapping 12-month holding windows. α annualised ×12. Significance: * 0.10 · ** 0.05 · *** 0.01.

Reproducibility. Full pipeline in nine independently runnable modules; run_all.py regenerates every figure here from raw data. src/validate.py asserts 15 anti-bias invariants, including an independent re-derivation of the selected group from formation data alone.
